## 2.1 What counts as RSI

Time: about 25 minutes

### Learn

Every Flex Online course has to show that the instructor is actively teaching. The federal term
is regular and substantive interaction (RSI). It comes from the U.S. Department of Education's
definition of distance education, and Texas A&M's credit hour rule adds that logging into a
course is not, by itself, teaching or learning. TLI checks for RSI in every Flex Online course
review. A course that cannot show it is treated as a correspondence course, and its students
lose eligibility for federal financial aid.

RSI has two tests, and a course has to pass both.

**Substantive** means the interaction is about teaching, learning or assessment. A reminder that
the case analysis is due Sunday is not substantive. An announcement explaining the concept half
the class missed on last week's quiz is.

**Regular** means predictable, proactive and responsive to how students are doing. Students
should know when they will hear from you, and you should notice when someone falls behind. Your
syllabus has to say how and when students can expect to hear from you. You will write that
statement in step 2.3.

For the substantive test, your course needs instructor activity in at least two of these five
categories:

| Category | Counts | Doesn't count on its own |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Direct instruction | Your recorded lectures, worked examples you narrate, case debriefs, guided questions | Third-party videos with no framing or follow-up, reading lists with no instructions, quizzes graded only automatically |
| 2. Assessing or giving feedback | Comments on graded work, audio or video feedback, feedback on drafts, peer review you oversee, AI-drafted feedback you review and edit | Scores only, generic comments such as "good job", peer grading you never see, AI feedback you didn't review |
| 3. Giving information or answering questions | Announcements that clear up a hard concept, content answers by email or in forums, module orientation videos that explain how the content fits together, Q&A sessions about content | Deadline reminders, automated messages, welcome videos with no later engagement, forums where only students answer |
| 4. Facilitating discussion | Follow-up questions, summaries that connect posts to the readings, live sessions about content | Posting a prompt and grading replies with no comment, office hours used only for logistics |
| 5. Other instructional activities | Feedback on journals, simulations you debrief, debriefs after group projects | Reflections nobody reads, simulations nobody discusses |

Most of the right-hand column isn't bad teaching. A good industry video helps students learn.
So does an auto-graded practice quiz. They count as RSI once you add your own engagement, such
as a two-minute introduction on what to watch for and a follow-up on what students noticed. The
fix often takes about ten minutes.

**Where AI fits.** AI can draft feedback, summarize discussion threads or flag students who have
gone quiet. The interaction counts when you review and adjust what goes to students. Feedback an
AI writes and you release unread does not. As AI takes on more of the explaining, what students
need most from you is the judgment it can't supply: framing the problem, weighing evidence,
and telling them where their reasoning went wrong (Maxwell, 2026).

What is enough depends on the course. A graduate seminar and a large introductory course look
different, even when both meet the standard.

### Draft (full build) and Review (refresh)

before seeing the answer. Not scored.

1. **The instructor posts a ten-minute industry video on the SBI feedback model, with no
   comment.**
   Answer: Doesn't meet RSI. Category 1 needs your framing or follow-up. Add a short
   introduction on what to watch for, then ask about it in the cohort session.

2. **After feedback scripts come in, the instructor posts an announcement: most scripts named
   the behavior but never said what should change. It includes one rewritten example.**
   Answer: Meets RSI, category 3. It responds to what students actually turned in and clears up
   a concept.

3. **Students complete an auto-graded scenario sort. The instructor checks completion.**
   Answer: Doesn't meet RSI on its own. Checking completion isn't teaching. It counts once you
   respond to the results, as the MGMT 615 instructor did.

4. **An AI tool drafts rubric comments on each script. The instructor reads each draft, edits
   about half, and releases them.**
   Answer: Meets RSI, category 2. AI-drafted feedback counts when you review and adjust it.
   Checking a handful of drafts and releasing the rest unread would not.

5. **Before the term starts, the instructor schedules eight Monday announcements listing that
   week's readings and due dates.**
   Answer: Doesn't meet RSI. The announcements are predictable, but they are about logistics,
   and messages written before the term can't respond to how students are doing.

## 2.2 Check one module for RSI

Time: about 25 minutes

### Learn

Now apply the two tests to your own course. Pick one module.

**Refresh track.** Choose a module you have taught more than once, since you know how students
respond to it. Pick two of its activities and decide whether each meets RSI as you teach it now.
Then choose one activity that doesn't, and describe the smallest change that would make it
count, including when in the week it happens. Every module has at least one.

**Full build.** Choose a module you are planning. A rough plan is fine; the point is to build
RSI in before any content exists. List three planned activities and decide whether each will
meet RSI. Check that they cover at least two categories. If they already do, name the activity
most likely to lose its RSI once the term gets busy, such as a discussion you plan to
facilitate but might end up only grading, and say how you will keep it.

Not every activity has to meet RSI. An auto-graded quiz can stay as practice. What matters is
that each module has instructor activity in at least two categories.

You will check every module's activities in Module 4. This step is practice on one.

### See it

The MGMT 615 instructor checked Module 3, Performance Management and Feedback. The feedback
script met RSI because the instructor reviews and edits every AI-drafted comment. The Perusall
annotation of the feedback chapter did not: students annotated, and the instructor only looked
at Perusall's scores. The upgrade was small. On Thursday, the instructor reads the most-discussed
passages and replies to two threads with a question that ties the chapter to Dana Ortiz's
conversation with Jordan. That takes about twenty minutes and makes the activity category 4.

## 2.3 Your syllabus statement on interaction

Time: about 20 minutes

### Learn

Your syllabus has to tell students how and when they will hear from you. Federal rules call
this regular interaction, and reviewers look for it.

A good statement covers three things in a sentence or two: how students will hear from you,
when, and how you will notice if someone falls behind. Fixed days work better than "regularly"
or "promptly." Keep it to something you will actually do in week ten of a busy term.

Example: "I'll post an announcement every Monday that responds to the previous week's work and
previews what's next. I review submissions each Wednesday, and if you've missed two activities,
I'll reach out to check in."

In Getting Started you described how students will hear from you in a normal week. This step
starts from that answer.

### See it

MGMT 615's statement: "Every Monday I'll post an announcement that picks up Dana's story, names
the week's decision and responds to what I saw in your last assignment. You'll get written
feedback on each module's main assignment within five days of the due date. I check activity
every Wednesday, and if you've missed a module's work, I'll contact you that week."

### Draft (full build)

## 2.4 The decision at the center of your story

Time: about 25 minutes

### Learn

The rest of the workshop builds a story that runs through your course: a central character, a
conflict and, in Module 3, one scenario per module. This step finds what the story is for.

A story earns a place in a course when it changes how students learn. Busy professionals rarely lack content. What they lack is a reason to care about this
concept now, and a concrete situation with people, stakes and uncertainty gives them one. "This
week: audit risk" becomes "Should this auditor sign off?" A story also gives students somewhere
to file what they learn. They remember how a framework worked under pressure better than they
remember the framework alone (Mayer, 2021).

Start with the performance. State in one sentence what students should be able to
do, taken from your CLOs:

- Evaluate whether internal controls over revenue can be relied on.
- Decide whether to launch a product on uncertain market data.
- Design a staffing plan that meets service levels at an acceptable cost.

If you can't say it in one sentence, the story will drift.

Then name the conflict that makes that performance hard. It should push directly against it.
Useful patterns:

- Conflicting goals, such as revenue targets against audit quality.
- Ambiguous data, such as forecasts that point in different directions.
- Misaligned incentives, such as a supervisor who wants the numbers to work.
- Time pressure that rewards the fast answer over the right one.

A good conflict is a judgment problem. If more information would settle it, it isn't a conflict
yet.

**Existing courses.** Many courses already have a throughline without calling it one: a company
that returns in several cases, a recurring client, a single long project. If yours does, the
story can grow from it.

### See it

MGMT 615's performance: "Diagnose why a team is underperforming before choosing a fix" (from
CLO 1). The conflict: the quick fix against the right fix. Six weeks before peak season, a
director has already blamed one shift lead, while the evidence points to a bonus that rewards
speed and a shift handoff nobody owns. Blaming the shift lead is fast and pleases the boss.
Fixing the causes takes longer and means questioning the boss's call.

### Draft (full build)

most often on the job?" and "Describe a moment from your field where a capable professional got
this decision wrong, or nearly did."

```text
the CLOs the conflict would put pressure on.

```

## 2.5 Build your character

Time: about 30 minutes

### Learn

The character is the person students follow through the course. You don't need a novelist's
protagonist. You need a believable decision-maker making the kind of calls your students will
make.

A good character:

- holds a role your students could have within a few years
- has enough authority to feel the weight of the decision, but not so much that the situation
  turns purely political
- faces the constraints your students will face, such as time pressure, incomplete information
  and incentives that pull the wrong way

The profile has six parts:

- **Premise statement**: "A story about [name, role] who struggles to [the performance from step
  2.4] when [the conflict]."
- **Position**: job title, organization, experience.
- **Conflict**: the conflict from step 2.4, made specific to this person.
- **Peers**: two to four colleagues who see the problem differently. They become the voices in
  your scenarios.
- **Values and fears**: what the character cares about and what they're afraid of. These are what
  make the decision hard.
- **Backstory**: a few lines on how they got here and why the problem matters to them.

**When one character doesn't fit.** Some courses cover areas no single person would own. You can
keep one setting and let a different character lead each module. You can keep one recurring
problem and change who faces it. Or you can use a narrator, such as a mentor or consultant, who
frames each module without making the decisions. Interdisciplinary courses usually do best with
the first. Survey courses can give each module its own short story and skip the course-wide
storyline.

**Your ID's role.** Your ID reviews your character and conflict at this module's check-in, before
you build scenarios on them in Module 3.

### See it

MGMT 615's premise statement: "A story about Dana Ortiz, a newly promoted operations manager at a
regional distribution center, who struggles to find the real causes of her site's slipping
performance and coach, delegate, communicate and hire her way to a fix when leadership has
already decided who to blame."

Dana leads three shift leads and about 45 associates. She was promoted eight months ago from
top-performing day-shift lead, and this is her first time managing managers. Her peers each see
the problem differently. Her night-shift lead agrees with the director, partly because the
broken handoff makes night shift look good. Her day-shift lead, Jordan, is capable but avoids
hard calls and is getting defensive. A manager at a sister site has the data that shows the
handoff problem but thinks Dana should replace Jordan anyway. Dana values fairness and remembers
what it was like to be managed badly. She fears pushing back on her director and being wrong.
